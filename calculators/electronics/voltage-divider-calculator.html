Voltage Divider Calculator – Resistor Output Voltage

Calculate the output voltage of a two-resistor voltage divider circuit. Find the correct R1 and R2 values to produce any desired output voltage from any input supply. Used in sensor interfaces, ADC references, and level shifting.

The voltage divider is perhaps the simplest analog circuit — just two resistors — yet it solves a remarkable variety of problems in electronics design. Signal level shifting, sensor biasing, reference voltage generation, and impedance matching all rely on the voltage divider principle. Mastering its design and limitations is fundamental to analog electronics.

Formula

Vout = Vin × R2 / (R1 + R2) | R1 = R2 × (Vin/Vout - 1)

Voltage Divider Circuit Guide

A voltage divider (also called a potential divider) uses two resistors in series to produce a fraction of the input voltage. It is one of the most fundamental and widely used circuits in electronics. Applications include sensor biasing, ADC input scaling, microcontroller level shifting (5V to 3.3V), and setting reference voltages.

How a Voltage Divider Works

With R1 connected from Vin to the output node, and R2 from the output node to ground, the output voltage follows: Vout = Vin × R2 / (R1 + R2). The voltage is divided in proportion to the resistor values. If R1 = R2, Vout = Vin/2.

Loading Effect — Critical Consideration

The voltage divider formula assumes no current is drawn from the output. When a load (like a microcontroller input) is connected, it creates a parallel resistance with R2, reducing Vout. For accurate division, the load resistance should be at least 10× R2. A low-impedance buffer (op-amp follower) eliminates loading effects.

Voltage Divider Applications in Electronics

Voltage dividers appear in virtually every electronic system. From sensor interfaces to ADC input scaling, from battery monitoring to audio volume controls — understanding when and how to use a voltage divider correctly prevents common design mistakes.

Battery Voltage Monitoring

Microcontrollers typically have 3.3V or 5V ADC inputs. To monitor a 12V lead-acid battery, a voltage divider scales it down: R1=27kΩ, R2=10kΩ gives Vout = 12×10/37 = 3.24V — within 3.3V ADC range. At full charge (14.4V): Vout = 3.89V — exceeds ADC range, so use R1=33kΩ for a safer scale. Always add protection diodes on ADC inputs.

NTC Thermistor Temperature Sensing

NTC (Negative Temperature Coefficient) thermistors are used in a voltage divider configuration. With a fixed resistor (equal to thermistor value at 25°C) and 3.3V supply: Vout varies from ~0.3V (hot) to ~3V (cold). The ADC reads Vout, the microcontroller calculates thermistor resistance, then uses Steinhart-Hart equation to find temperature. Used in printers, 3D printers, and HVAC systems.

Audio Volume Control

A potentiometer is a variable voltage divider. Rotating the wiper changes the ratio R2/(R1+R2), scaling the audio signal voltage. Log-taper potentiometers (audio taper) are used for volume controls because human hearing perceives loudness logarithmically — a linear taper pot sounds like most volume change happens at the top of the dial.

Frequently Asked Questions

How does a voltage divider work? +
Two resistors in series split the input voltage. Vout = Vin × R2 / (R1 + R2). Current flows through both resistors equally (series circuit). The output is taken from the junction between R1 and R2. Larger R2 relative to R1 gives higher Vout.
How do I level shift 5V to 3.3V with a voltage divider? +
Use R1 = 10kΩ and R2 = 20kΩ: Vout = 5 × 20/(10+20) = 5 × 0.667 = 3.33V. Suitable for connecting 5V microcontroller outputs to 3.3V device inputs. Add a buffer if the load is low-impedance.
What is the loading effect on a voltage divider? +
When a load is connected across R2, it reduces the effective resistance, lowering Vout. To minimize loading, keep R1 and R2 small compared to the load impedance, or use an op-amp voltage follower buffer after the divider.
How do I calculate R1 and R2 for a desired output voltage? +
R1 = R2 × (Vin/Vout - 1). Choose R2 first based on power consumption and loading requirements (typically 1kΩ-100kΩ), then calculate R1. For 5V input, 3.3V output, R2=10kΩ: R1 = 10k × (5/3.3 - 1) = 10k × 0.515 = 5.15kΩ → use 5.1kΩ.
Can a voltage divider supply current to a load? +
Voltage dividers are inefficient for supplying current. They are designed for signal-level connections, not power delivery. A linear voltage regulator or DC-DC converter is far more efficient when you need to supply current to a load.
What resistor values should I use for a voltage divider? +
Choose resistor values based on: power consumption (P = Vin²/(R1+R2)), loading effects (load should be >10× R2), and available standard values. For 3.3V logic: 10kΩ and 20kΩ are common. Lower values waste more power but handle loading better.
