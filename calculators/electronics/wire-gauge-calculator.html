Wire Gauge Calculator – AWG, Current Capacity and Voltage Drop

Find the correct wire gauge (AWG) for any current load and calculate voltage drop over cable runs. Convert between AWG and metric mm² cross-sectional area. Essential for electrical wiring, automotive, solar panel, and speaker cable selection.

Correct wire gauge selection is one of the most safety-critical decisions in any electrical installation. Undersized wiring is a leading cause of residential electrical fires — the wire overheats under excess current, melts insulation, and ignites surrounding materials. The National Electrical Code (NEC) provides minimum wire gauge requirements, but understanding the underlying calculations helps make informed, safe choices.

Wire Gauge Selection Guide

Selecting the correct wire gauge is critical for electrical safety and performance. Undersized wire causes dangerous overheating, voltage drop, and fire hazard. The American Wire Gauge (AWG) system uses an inverse numbering system — larger AWG numbers mean thinner wire. In metric systems, wire is specified by cross-sectional area in mm².

AWG System Explained

The AWG scale goes from 0000 (4/0, the thickest practical wire) down to 40 (hair-thin wire used in electronics). Each 3-step decrease in AWG doubles the wire area and roughly doubles current capacity. Each single AWG step changes resistance by about 26%. Common household wiring uses 14 AWG (15A circuits) and 12 AWG (20A circuits).

Wire Selection for Different Applications

Choosing the right wire gauge involves more than just current capacity. Wire material, insulation type, installation method, ambient temperature, and bundling all affect the safe current-carrying capacity. The NEC (National Electrical Code) provides detailed tables for different installation scenarios.

Copper vs Aluminum Wiring

Copper is the standard for most wiring: lower resistance, easier to terminate, and more flexible. Aluminum is used for large service entrances and branch circuits (cheaper, lighter). Aluminum has 61% the conductivity of copper — aluminum must be 2 AWG sizes larger for the same current. Never mix copper and aluminum without proper connectors — galvanic corrosion causes dangerous loose connections.

NEC Ampacity Derating

NEC Table 310.15 specifies wire ampacity based on temperature rating and installation. Key derating rules: in conduit with 4+ current-carrying conductors, derate to 80%. In ambient temperatures above 30°C, derate further. For continuous loads (over 3 hours), use only 80% of breaker rating. A 20A breaker with continuous load: max 16A, requiring 12 AWG rated at 20A.

Voltage Drop in DC Systems

Voltage drop is critical in 12V DC systems (automotive, solar, RV). A 3% drop on 12V = only 0.36V — motors run hotter, LEDs are dimmer, microcontrollers may reset. For 12V systems, target under 2% drop. Calculate: Vdrop = 2 × Length × Current × (Resistance/meter). A 10m run of 10 AWG at 20A: 2 × 10 × 20 × 0.00328 = 1.31V drop (10.9% — too much, upgrade to 8 AWG).

Frequently Asked Questions

What wire gauge do I need for 20 amps? +
For 20A circuits, use 12 AWG wire minimum (US NEC code requires 12 AWG for 20A branch circuits). For longer runs over 50 feet, consider upgrading to 10 AWG to keep voltage drop below 3%.
What is the difference between AWG and mm²? +
AWG is the American Wire Gauge system (larger number = thinner wire). mm² is the European/metric system measuring cross-sectional area (larger number = thicker wire). Common conversions: 14 AWG ≈ 2.08mm², 12 AWG ≈ 3.31mm², 10 AWG ≈ 5.26mm².
How do I calculate voltage drop in a wire? +
Voltage drop = 2 × Length (m) × Current (A) × Resistance per meter (Ω/m). Factor of 2 accounts for both conductors (live and neutral). Use online tables for resistance per meter — 12 AWG copper has about 5.21 mΩ/m.
What happens if wire gauge is too small? +
Undersized wire causes: overheating (possible fire), excessive voltage drop (motors overheat, lights dim), nuisance tripping of circuit breakers, and insulation damage over time. Always use the minimum recommended gauge or larger.
What AWG wire is used for solar panels? +
Solar panel strings typically use 10 AWG (5.26mm²) for runs under 50 feet. For longer home runs, 8 AWG or 6 AWG may be needed to keep voltage drop below 2-3%. Solar wire (USE-2 or PV wire) is rated for outdoor/UV exposure.
How do I convert AWG to mm²? +
mm² = (diameter in mm)² × π/4. AWG diameter (mm) = 0.127 × 92^((36-AWG)/39). Or use the approximation: mm² ≈ 35/(2^(AWG/6)) for common sizes. 12 AWG ≈ 3.3mm², 10 AWG ≈ 5.3mm², 8 AWG ≈ 8.4mm².
