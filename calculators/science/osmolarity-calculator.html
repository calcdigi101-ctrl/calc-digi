Tonicity and Cells

Isotonic: no net water movement. Hypotonic (lower): water enters cells → swell, may lyse. Hypertonic (higher): water leaves → cells shrink. IV fluids must match desired effect.

Dissociation Factors

NaCl→Na⁺+Cl⁻: i=2. CaCl₂→Ca²⁺+2Cl⁻: i=3. Glucose (non-electrolyte): i=1. Each ion contributes independently.

Osmolarity in Physiology and Clinical Medicine

Osmolarity is one of the most clinically important laboratory parameters. It determines fluid shifts between compartments, affects cell volume, drives thirst and antidiuretic hormone (ADH) release, and is central to the understanding and treatment of dehydration, fluid overload, and poisoning with osmotically active substances like ethanol and ethylene glycol.

Osmolarity Regulation in the Body

Blood osmolarity is tightly regulated at 285-295 mOsm/L by the hypothalamus-kidney-ADH axis. Increased osmolarity: hypothalamus osmoreceptors sense high osmolarity → ADH released from posterior pituitary → kidneys concentrate urine (up to 1200 mOsm/L) → water retained → osmolarity restored. Decreased osmolarity: ADH suppressed → kidneys dilute urine (down to 50 mOsm/L) → water excreted → osmolarity restored. This feedback loop operates continuously to maintain fluid balance within ±2-3% of set point.

Calculated vs Measured Osmolality

Serum osmolality can be calculated from major solutes: Calculated Osm = 2×Na⁺ + Glucose(mg/dL)/18 + BUN(mg/dL)/2.8. The factor 2 for sodium accounts for associated anions (mainly Cl⁻ and HCO₃⁻). Normal: 285-295 mOsm/kg. Osmolal gap = Measured - Calculated. Normal gap ≤10 mOsm/kg. Elevated gap indicates unmeasured osmoles: ethanol (divide ethanol mg/dL by 4.6), methanol, ethylene glycol (antifreeze), isopropanol, mannitol. Osmolal gap calculation is a key tool in toxicology to detect and quantify ingested alcohols.

Parenteral Nutrition and IV Fluids

Intravenous fluid selection requires matching osmolarity to clinical goals. Isotonic fluids (0.9% NaCl, 308 mOsm/L; Lactated Ringers, 273 mOsm/L): expand extracellular volume without causing cell swelling or shrinkage — used for volume resuscitation. Hypotonic fluids (0.45% NaCl, 154 mOsm/L; D5W, 252 mOsm/L): free water distributes to all compartments — used for free water replacement. Hypertonic fluids (3% NaCl, 1028 mOsm/L; 20% mannitol): draw water from cells — used for cerebral edema. Total parenteral nutrition: 1800-2400 mOsm/L — must be administered through a central venous catheter to avoid peripheral vein damage.

Osmolarity, Tonicity and Osmotic Pressure – Complete Clinical Guide

Osmolarity measures the total concentration of solute particles in a solution, expressed in milliosmoles per liter (mOsm/L). Unlike molarity which counts only one type of molecule, osmolarity counts every dissolved particle — including all ions from electrolyte dissociation. NaCl dissociates into Na⁺ + Cl⁻ (2 particles), so 1 mM NaCl = 2 mOsm/L. Glucose does not dissociate, so 1 mM glucose = 1 mOsm/L. The total osmolarity is the sum of contributions from all dissolved species.

Osmolarity is critical in clinical medicine because it determines the movement of water across semipermeable membranes (osmosis) and therefore controls cell volume, blood pressure, kidney function, and fluid balance throughout the body. Mismatched osmolarity between an IV fluid and blood plasma causes red blood cells to swell or shrink — with potentially fatal consequences.

Calculating Serum Osmolarity – Clinical Formula

Serum osmolality is estimated clinically: Calculated Osm = 2 × [Na⁺] + [Glucose (mg/dL)]/18 + [BUN (mg/dL)]/2.8. The factor of 2 for sodium accounts for the associated anions (mainly Cl⁻ and HCO₃⁻). Normal: 285-295 mOsm/kg. Example: Na⁺ = 140 mEq/L, Glucose = 90 mg/dL, BUN = 14 mg/dL. Calculated Osm = 2×140 + 90/18 + 14/2.8 = 280 + 5 + 5 = 290 mOsm/kg. Osmolal gap = Measured (by freezing-point osmometry) - Calculated. Normal gap ≤ 10 mOsm/kg. Elevated gap suggests unmeasured osmoles: ethanol (add ethanol mg/dL ÷ 4.6 to close the gap), methanol, ethylene glycol (antifreeze poisoning), isopropanol, mannitol.

IV Fluid Tonicity and Clinical Decision Making

IV fluid selection depends on the clinical goal: Isotonic fluids (osmolarity close to plasma 285-295 mOsm/L): expand extracellular volume without causing net water movement into or out of cells. 0.9% NaCl (308 mOsm/L), Lactated Ringer's (273 mOsm/L), PlasmaLyte (294 mOsm/L). Used for volume resuscitation in hemorrhage, dehydration, and septic shock. Hypotonic fluids (osmolarity < 285 mOsm/L): free water distributes into cells. 0.45% NaCl (154 mOsm/L), D5W (252 mOsm/L — glucose metabolized rapidly, leaving essentially free water). Used for free water replacement in hypernatremia. Hypertonic fluids (osmolarity > 295 mOsm/L): draw water from cells. 3% NaCl (1028 mOsm/L): symptomatic hyponatremia, cerebral edema from head trauma. 20% mannitol: acute cerebral edema, glaucoma.

Osmosis and Tonicity – Cell Biology

Cell membranes are selectively permeable — permeable to water but not to most solutes. Water moves across membranes by osmosis from lower to higher osmolarity (thermodynamically from high water concentration to low). Isotonic solution (same osmolarity as cell): no net osmotic water movement, cells maintain normal volume. Hypotonic solution (lower osmolarity than cell): water enters cells by osmosis → cells swell. Red blood cells in pure water: swell and lyse (hemolysis). Hypertonic solution (higher osmolarity): water leaves cells by osmosis → cells shrink (crenation, plasmolysis in plants). Clinical significance: giving hypotonic fluid too rapidly can cause cerebral edema as brain cells swell — correcting hyponatremia should proceed at maximum 10-12 mEq/L per 24 hours to avoid osmotic demyelination syndrome.

Osmolarity in Parenteral Nutrition

Total Parenteral Nutrition (TPN) is a hypertonic solution (typically 1800-2400 mOsm/L) containing amino acids, glucose, lipids, electrolytes, vitamins, and trace elements for patients unable to eat. Osmolarity above 900 mOsm/L must be administered through a central venous catheter (subclavian or jugular vein) because hypertonic solutions damage peripheral veins (thrombophlebitis, chemical burn to vein endothelium). Peripheral Parenteral Nutrition (PPN) is limited to ≤900 mOsm/L to protect peripheral veins — provides less caloric density. Osmolarity calculation guides catheter selection and prevents serious complications in critically ill patients receiving nutritional support.

Osmolarity in Sports Science and Hydration

Sweat osmolarity: 10-70 mOsm/L (hypotonic compared to plasma) — predominantly water loss with some electrolytes. Sports drinks are formulated isotonic to slightly hypotonic (200-330 mOsm/L): fast gastric emptying for rapid absorption, replaces electrolytes. Pure water during prolonged exercise causes exercise-associated hyponatremia (EAH) — diluting serum sodium below 135 mEq/L, causing nausea, confusion, and seizures in severe cases. Classic example: marathon runners who drink only water develop dilutional hyponatremia. Guideline: drink to thirst during exercise, replace electrolytes in events >1 hour, avoid drinking more than you sweat.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is osmolarity? +
Total solute particle concentration (mOsm/L). NaCl (i=2): 1 mM=2 mOsm. Glucose (i=1): 1 mM=1 mOsm.
Normal blood osmolarity? +
285-295 mOsm/L. Below 275=hypo-osmolality (brain edema). Above 320=altered mental status.
Osmolarity vs osmolality? +
Osmolarity: mOsm/L (volume-based). Osmolality: mOsm/kg water (mass-based). Clinically measured = osmolality.
Normal saline osmolarity? +
0.9% NaCl=154 mM. NaCl→2 ions: 154×2=308 mOsm/L.
Osmolal gap? +
Measured - calculated osmolality. Normal <10. Elevated suggests ethanol, methanol, or glycols.
Osmotic pressure? +
π=iCRT. Blood ~7.7 atm. Maintains fluid within vessels. Low albumin→reduced oncotic pressure→edema.
