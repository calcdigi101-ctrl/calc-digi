Frequently Asked Questions

How do I calculate my annual business revenue? +
Annual Revenue = Sum of all revenue-generating activities for 12 months. For product businesses: Units Sold × Average Selling Price. For subscriptions: Monthly Customers × Monthly Price × 12. For services: Billable Hours × Hourly Rate. For e-commerce: Number of Orders × Average Order Value. Total revenue = all revenue streams combined.
What is MRR and ARR? +
MRR (Monthly Recurring Revenue) = total predictable revenue per month from active subscriptions. ARR (Annual Recurring Revenue) = MRR × 12. These metrics apply to subscription businesses. $50,000 MRR = $600,000 ARR. MRR growth rate = key health metric: (Current MRR - Previous MRR) / Previous MRR × 100. 10-15% monthly MRR growth = hypergrowth.
What is a good revenue growth rate for a startup? +
T2D3 rule (popular SaaS benchmark): Triple revenue in years 1-2, Double in years 3-5. Year 1: $1M → $3M. Year 2: $3M → $9M. Year 3: $9M → $18M. Year 4: $18M → $36M. Year 5: $36M → $72M. Reaching $100M ARR in 7-10 years at this pace. Most startups fall short — 20-50% annual growth in years 3-5 is still healthy.
How do I project future revenue? +
Simple projection: Future Revenue = Current Revenue × (1 + Monthly Growth Rate)^Months. At 10% monthly growth, $50,000 current MRR projects to $50,000 × (1.10)^12 = $156,920 in 12 months. More sophisticated: build a bottoms-up model from leads, conversion rates, and deal sizes. Top-down models (market share projections) are less reliable for early-stage companies.
What is the Rule of 40 for SaaS businesses? +
Rule of 40: Revenue Growth Rate % + Profit Margin % should equal or exceed 40. A SaaS company growing 30% with 15% profit margin: 30 + 15 = 45 — above 40, healthy. A company growing 60% with -15% margin: 60 + (-15) = 45 — also healthy. Above 40 indicates a well-balanced growth and profitability tradeoff. Used by investors to evaluate SaaS company health.
What is gross revenue vs net revenue? +
Gross Revenue: total revenue before any deductions (all sales at full price). Net Revenue: gross revenue minus returns, discounts, and allowances. Discounts, promotional pricing, and return allowances are deducted to arrive at net revenue. Net revenue is what appears on the income statement. Gross and net revenue are important for understanding customer satisfaction (high returns = product issues) and pricing strategy effectiveness.
