How Investment Growth is Calculated

Investment growth combines two powerful forces: compound interest on your initial investment and the cumulative effect of regular contributions over time.

Future Value = P(1+r/n)^(nt) + PMT × [((1+r/n)^(nt) − 1) / (r/n)]

Where P = initial investment, r = annual return rate, n = compounding frequency, t = years, and PMT = periodic contribution.

The earlier you start investing, the more time compound growth has to work. A 25-year-old investing $300/month at 7% return will have significantly more at retirement than a 35-year-old investing the same amount, purely due to the extra 10 years of compounding.

How much will my investment grow in 10 years? +
It depends on your initial amount, contributions, and return rate. For example, $10,000 invested at 7% with no additional contributions grows to about $19,672 in 10 years. Adding $200/month brings the total to over $54,000.
What is a realistic investment return to expect? +
The S&P 500 has averaged about 10% annually historically (7% after inflation). Most financial planners use 6-8% as a conservative long-term planning assumption for diversified stock portfolios.
Should I invest a lump sum or contribute monthly? +
Lump sum investing historically outperforms dollar-cost averaging about 2/3 of the time because markets trend upward over time. However, monthly contributions reduce risk and are more practical for most people building wealth from income.
How does inflation affect my investment returns? +
Inflation erodes purchasing power. A 7% nominal return with 3% inflation yields roughly 4% real return. Always consider "real" (inflation-adjusted) returns when planning long-term financial goals like retirement.
